# Niche and Branding

Social media does not let you post whatever you want and succeed. That is not how any other professional domain works, and content creation is no exception.

## Core Principles

### You Are NOT the Niche (Yet)

This is the most important reframe in the entire framework. The "you are the niche" advice circulating online only applies to roughly 1% of people - those with a 10/10 personality and exceptional natural charisma.

The selfishness problem sits at the heart of this. The biggest mistake creators make is selfishness: asking why audiences won't watch their videos, or deciding they're tired of their niche and want to talk about themselves, or wondering when they can become the niche. The framework is blunt in response: where else in life does someone succeed doing whatever they want? If a quarterback told the coach he was just going to do whatever he felt like that day, or if Pizza Hut decided on Tuesdays it would be a bookstore - neither would survive. Content works the same way.

The reason creators believe they should be the niche: they've been sold the lie by influencers who do "day in the life" content. That privilege is not available early. Accept it.

### The Amazon Blueprint for Niche Expansion

Instead of trying to be the niche from day one, follow the Amazon blueprint - a three-stage process that maps directly to creator growth:

**Stage 1 - Crack Into the Market (0-10K followers)**:
What was Amazon's very first product? Books. Just books. Jeff Bezos knew the whole time he would deliver everything eventually, but he cracked into the delivery ecosystem with a single product category.

For creators: be hyper-niched, be specific, crack in with a counter-position. Nobody cares about building in public or day-in-the-life or talking about yourself. At this stage, you are earning the right to exist in the market.

**Stage 2 - Expand (10K-250K followers)**:
Amazon after books moved to CDs, music, and movies. They mastered that. One main niche with a few extras.

For creators: now it's possible to get a little creative and add a layer - one or two angles, a couple of different perspectives. This is where personality gets added without abandoning the core.

**Stage 3 - Transcend (250K+ followers)**:
Amazon went from selling books and CDs to literally selling everything. This is the Hormozi level, the TJR level. They've conquered their niche and can now expand through it.

For creators: ONLY at this stage can you talk about whatever you want. You've conquered your niche, you're extremely wealthy, you have a team, and you have half a million followers. That privilege doesn't exist before this. Stick to the blueprints.

### The Niche Expansion Through Volume (TJR Model)

At the extreme end of Stage 3, creators like TJR post 40-60 times per day. He has transcended his niche and has a long-form channel. He can post general content because his overall reach drives people to long form, which converts. But this is a rare occasion reserved for creators who have cracked their niche and capped it out.

The progression example of Iman Gadzhi: started in dropshipping, moved to business, then to more lifestyle content, then to general success. He did this through volume but also by maxing out each level before moving up. He didn't jump straight to success content from the bottom.

### Finding Your Niche - The Three-Question Blueprint

Three questions from Matthew McConaughey's Diary of a CEO appearance serve as a useful niche-finding framework:

**Question 1: Is it in your DNA?**
Look at your story, your childhood, your natural giftings, how you've impacted other people. Is your niche woven into your history? Wrestling, football, communication, creative problem-solving - many things could be a niche. The question is which one you took action on.

**Question 2: Can you outwork and outlast everyone in it?**
You don't know for sure. You don't know where burnout is. But is it something you're passionate about enough to work harder for longer? This is critical because if you're already thinking about pivoting in 3-6 months, it's not your niche.

On the "I don't want to pigeonhole myself" crowd: that thinking needs to be set aside. Pigeonhole yourself. It's the only way to build compounding results.

**Question 3: Can you make a living off of it?**
This maps to the niche quality quadrant. Services to rich people equals a financially savvy niche. Products to poor people is a harder path. If the niche cannot support a high-ticket service, that's fine - just be aware of the ceiling.

Reframing niche: it's not a niche, it's a calling. What do you want on your gravestone? Pick something you'd run into the ground for the next 40-50 years. That's the durability test.

### Finding Your Niche Through Action

The book "Mastery" by Robert Greene is the top recommendation for niche discovery. The core thesis: you find your life's work from taking action. It will often find you.

This reframes the entire anxiety around choosing a niche. It is not a one-time decision you agonize over. It is revealed through doing. The answer to "what is my niche?" is action, not contemplation.

### The Passion vs. Monetization Reality

You have to pick your poison: financial freedom or pure creativity. The sweet spot exists, but you reach it by going through monetization first, then weaving creativity back in.

Steve Jobs probably didn't care most deeply about designing computers in a dream world. He wanted to do more artistic things. But what he did was go through the process of becoming a great CEO of a computer company and then wove his creativity back into it.

The explicit trade: in your first 100 days, your first year, your first three years - bite the bullet, do things that are a little less creative, make money. Sacrifice some creativity now, gain the financial freedom to be more creative later. Creators who chase pure creativity before money often quit because they can't sustain it financially.

## Brand Pillars Framework

### What Brand Pillars Are

Don't just pick a niche - pick three brand pillars. This is the system used with major clients in Navy SEAL, bodybuilder, and biohacker niches. Everyone tries to replicate where successful creators are now, but that won't work. Look at what they did at the beginning of their creator careers.

### Why Just a Niche Is Not Enough

A niche alone means you don't stand out. You don't counter-position inside your niche. You have no association to people outside your niche. Why would someone follow you if you're just another fitness creator doing fitness, fitness, fitness?

### The Three Pillars

**Pillar 1 - Core Pillar (must make people money)**:
Your first pillar needs to be attached to something that makes other people money, if you're building a business on your creator platform. Think tax strategist, email marketing, remote sales, helping people get jobs.

The gardening example: "I want to be in the gardening niche" is not a great start because it's hard to monetize. But level it up - connect it to locally sourced foods for restaurant farm-to-table supply. Now you're helping restaurants access a revenue-relevant service. The fitness example: go one layer deeper - how gyms can monetize with saunas.

**Pillar 2 - Personality Pillar 1 (something you genuinely like)**:
Pokemon cards, Peloton, hopscotch - things you actually enjoy. These give someone a different angle into your personality and make you more than your core offer.

**Pillar 3 - Personality Pillar 2 (another interest that gives association)**:
Another genuine interest. For the gardening example: Pokemon cards and Peloton as personality pillars.

### How It Works Together

The core pillar builds the business. The personality pillars surround the core offer and give people association. A gardening creator who also talks about Pokemon cards will attract people who think: "I like Pokemon cards and I'm a restaurant owner" or "I like Peloton and I'm in farming." People follow for the core value but connect through the personality pillars.

## Counter-Positioning - The Number One Skill

Counter-positioning is not just a content tactic - it is the number one skill in marketing, period. It has an underlying master skill: pattern interruption.

To be a great creator, you have to understand that counter-positioning has pattern interruption as its foundation.

### The Liquid Death Case Study

In 2018, the water bottle industry looked like this: Aquafina 13%, Dasani 9%, Smart Water 7%, Fiji 2%, Evian 2%. Liquid Death was just founded.

Seven years later in 2025: Aquafina down 1%, Smart Water down 1%, Fiji down 1%. Liquid Death went UP 3% - putting them ranked number four in the entire water bottle industry. That 3% would translate to roughly a billion views a year in content terms.

The lesson: Liquid Death gave the blueprint to succeed on content and it is NOT copying people. Copying is a losing strategy because market cycles in content are moving so fast, people are getting burnt out on the same formats and styles.

### Three Ways to Counter-Position

**Method 1: Add something to your niche**
Horse riding + AI. You stand out in both worlds. This is cross-niche flavoring - importing something unexpected from one world into another.

Andrew Huberman example: self-improvement niche, but he added science. That is a cross-niche counter-position.
Shelby Stapp example: sales niche, but she is a girl. Gender, age, and race can all be counter-positions.

**Method 2: Go upstream or downstream**
Talk to rich people (upstream) or beginners (downstream). Horse riding for rich people, OR horse riding for college students. Find a pocket of your niche that is underserved.

**Method 3: Counter-position with association**
Huberman is in self-improvement, but he's associated with science. Nike is in athletics, but they're associated with killers - Kobe Bryant, Michael Jordan - not like Lululemon.

The questions to ask: Who do you quote? How do you communicate? Do you use math? Do you reference Steve Jobs? All of that is association.

### Real-World Counter-Positioning Examples

- **Blue M&M packaging**: Stands out in the candy aisle against all the red, yellow, and brown. If you want to see great marketing, go to the candy aisle at Target and let your eyes hit it. These are basically YouTube thumbnails - the concept of packaging.
- **"Stop. Do not buy from us" email**: Out of everyone promoting sales and discounts, this email said stop. Why? Because tomorrow they were running a sale, and they said wait today. Counter-positioned against every other promotional email.
- **Shelby Stapp**: A girl in the high-ticket sales niche. You can counter-position with gender, age, and race alone.
- **Omaha vs. Miami**: The personal brand niche is filled with Miami gurus and sick cars. A creator with a modest setup and "poor ceiling" living in Omaha, Nebraska tells people something is more trustworthy - whether or not that's objectively true.

### The Pendulum Swing

The pendulum is swinging away from Lambos, helicopters, and dollar amounts - people are getting tired of it and swinging back the opposite direction. This happens with any psychological medium: fashion, families, even politics. The opportunity is always on the opposite side of the current trend.

## Pattern Interruption in Branding

Counter-positioning IS pattern interruption applied to your brand. Three specific tactical approaches:

### 1. Change Your Format

A format is the visual that people are seeing. If your niche is full of people yapping in cars, do green screen. If it's full of green screen, do a vlog. If everyone does gym content lifting in mirrors, do object lessons.

Jeff Magnuson is the prime example: he does object lessons in the fitness niche where everyone else lifts shirtless in mirrors. He is effectively pattern interrupting and counter-positioning the entire fitness niche with format alone.

### 2. Find a Better Niche Pocket

Go luxury (upstream), go beginner (downstream), or add a cross-niche flavor. This is not about changing your niche - it's about finding the underserved corner of it.

### 3. Counter-Position with Branding/Messaging

This is what Liquid Death did. Your visual identity, your tone, your entire brand presentation can be the counter-position. A modest Omaha setup versus a flashy Miami guru aesthetic IS a counter-position. It communicates authenticity through contrast.

## Value-Driven vs. Entertainment-Driven Creators

### The Five Categories of Uniqueness

Every piece of content falls into one of five categories. Within any category, uniqueness is the prerequisite. Without uniqueness, none of them work.

**1. Unique Value (strong)**:
Actionable tips nobody else gives. One of the main reasons people don't get followers from giving advice is that the advice isn't unique enough. If you repeat what Alex Hormozi has said, why would someone follow you? They already have that follower slot filled.

**2. Unique Perspective (strong)**:
Can you blow someone's mind on how they think about the world? Can you change their worldview through perspective? It might not be actionable tips, but it's a new way of thinking about something.

**3. Unique Humor (rare)**:
Most creators aren't funny. Most should not try this. But if the humor is genuinely unique, it's a powerful category.

**4. Empathy (harder than you think)**:
Relatable shared experience. An amputee making content about having one arm will attract other amputees. The key is that the shared experience must be genuinely unique and specific.

**5. Aspirational Content (WORST)**:
This is the weakest category. Most lives are not uniquely aspirational. A day-in-the-life going to a normal gym eating normal food, relying on a mildly decent body, will not work. Millions of people have mildly decent bodies online. The only time aspirational works: if the aspiration is genuinely unique.

### The View-to-Follower Ratio Insight

A video with 200,000 views got about 500 followers, while a video with 70,000 views (less than half) got double the followers. The difference was uniqueness. The high-view video was broad; the high-conversion video was deeply unique.

### The Quiz: What Matters Most?

Five options for being a great creator - expert in your field, most unique, best communicator, wealthiest, or hottest. There's only one correct answer: unique.

If you are the number one Harvard research assistant but cannot communicate more uniquely than everyone else, they won't follow you. That's the difficult truth about social media - people follow unique communicators, not verified experts. The silver lining: being unique with even a little communication skill is enough. Greg Eisenberg is not an exceptional communicator in the traditional sense, but he's genuinely unique in how he positions himself.

## Be Great, Not Just Attention-Seeking

### The Hierarchy of Dilution

Jimmy Iovine (associated with Dr. Dre, Eminem, John Lennon, Bruce Springsteen, Beats by Dre, Steve Jobs) articulated it clearly: "People don't want to be great anymore. They just want attention."

The devolution:

1. **Great** (before the 2000s) - the concept of a craftsman
2. **Money** - the craftsman concept went out, people just wanted to get rich
3. **Famous** - when Hollywood really started to pop off
4. **Attention** - now people don't even want to be famous. They just want attention.

The world has become corny because everyone's doing the stupidest stuff for a few seconds of attention.

### The Plot Twist

If you just tried to be great at what you do, you would get more attention. If you just tried to be the best fitness coach of all time, you would get more attention. If you just tried to be the best AI consultant of all time, you would get more attention.

### How to Become Great

Three steps:

1. **Don't be afraid to work for someone exceptional in your niche.** Nine months working under someone exceptional accelerates the entire trajectory.
2. **Go back to books.** Read phenomenal books in your domain.
3. **Take extremely fast action and get feedback loops from your action.** Most people have big ideas they never execute. They never learn from inaction.

And then: do it for a long time and don't quit. Stop changing your niche. You will never be great if you keep switching. You will lose all your compounding results.

## How to Say No on the Come Up

### The Selfishness Problem Expanded

This point appears across the framework repeatedly because it is the most common mistake: prioritizing what you want to talk about over what your audience needs from you. The urge to expand too early is essentially selfishness dressed up as creative ambition.

### Don't Listen to People Who Haven't Hit Your Target

When building a strategy to compete against a dominant player in a niche, the blueprint is: counter-position against the big dog, never repurpose content (everything native), and keep costs extremely low.

On copying big creators: anyone who says "don't post daily" is wrong. Anyone who says "always post as much as possible" is also wrong. It depends on where you are. If someone is giving non-nuanced advice, don't listen to them about anything.

## The Fitness Niche Case Study (Bearer of Bad News)

The fitness niche is the go-to example of everything that can go wrong:

**The worst-enemy blueprint applied to fitness**: Pick fitness with zero uniqueness. Classic recipes, workouts, "how I get my biceps." Everything self-centered. 80% lifestyle content documenting an eight-pound cut or winter bulk. No value given. Avoid peptides because they're "too interesting right now." Monetize with a low-ticket Skool community. Result: 20,000 fake followers and 100 views.

**The counter-position opportunity in fitness**: Jeff Magnuson does object lessons where everyone else lifts shirtless in mirrors. That is the fitness counter-position. Format alone can set you apart when the entire niche uses the same visual language.

**The monetization reality in fitness**: Fitness sits in the top-left quadrant - loosely connected to money but hard to offer a high-ticket service. "You can pay me a hundred bucks and might have a more fit life theoretically." But with creative thinking: calling gyms locally and offering a $1,000 rehabilitation course turns it into a B2B high-ticket service.

## Expanding Your Niche Over Time

### The Accordion Approach to Niche Expansion

The accordion method applies to niche scope just as it applies to posting frequency:

1. **Compress**: Start hyper-niched. One topic, one format. Iterate.
2. **Expand**: Once you crack 3K followers and 3-5K views, you can add personality layers. One or two extra brand pillars.
3. **Compress**: If you hit a plateau at 50K followers, go back and find new depth within the existing niche. Don't expand - go deeper.
4. **Expand**: After breaking through with renewed depth and quality, NOW expand scope. Add new content categories.

The key: you only expand after earning the right by mastering the previous level.

### How to Crack Into a Market (The Market-Dethroning Blueprint)

An actual blueprint for a client trying to take market share from a dominant player:

**Step 1 - Counter-position against the big dog**: A market leader talks about working hard, building business, going all-in. That might represent only 30% of the niche. The other 70% wants lifestyle entrepreneurship - how to make a million dollars and only work 10 hours, travel, spend time with family. Take the counter approach.

**Step 2 - Never repurpose content**: Market leaders sometimes use AI to clone voice and lips. Over time that creates follower fatigue. The strategy: never repurpose content. Everything native and through volume.

**Step 3 - Keep costs extremely low**: A market leader with 18-20 people on a media team spends roughly a million a year. Counter-strategy: use AI animations, run a slim team, try to outlast them. This is a 5-7 year play.

The business parallel: Mailchimp had less than 50 employees, was profitable from the start, and survived while 12 VC-funded email service providers tried to beat them. They sold for $19 billion. Keep costs low, keep profit margins high, withstand time - when the giants stumble, that's when you explode.

## Personal Brand Building Through Detachment

### What Makes a 10/10 Yapper

Great "yappers" - people who can grab the phone with no edits and drop a 100K video - share three traits:

**1. Reps**: It probably takes 100 yaps to just get your personality across cleanly. A client who had scripted their entire career built a massive following but no cult following. When they started yapping twice a day, they improved dramatically within six weeks. Many great yappers had public communication backgrounds - teachers, public speakers - or they ground through hundreds of attempts.

**2. Communication tactics in their bones**: They know how to structure a video, build curiosity, and it flows naturally. This comes from practice, but also from learning and studying communicators. Reading books about psychological principles. Listening to great speakers and analyzing how they told stories. Over time, these tactics become internalized - you don't have to write them down, they just come out.

**3. Deeply secure in who they are**: The one nobody talks about. A yap is very vulnerable. You have no idea if the world is going to like it - it's the purest form of who you are, no scripting, no editing, just grip it and rip it. Great yappers can detach from metrics, which keeps them in the game, keeps volume high, and lets them iterate endlessly.

### The Five Detachment Traps

Five things that cause creators to stop posting during a phase of growth:

1. **Thinking old ideas are stale**: David Ogilvy said we don't market to a standing army - we make content to a marching crowd. People are always coming and going. Only 15% of current followers see both posts. You can revisit ideas every 2-4 weeks.

2. **Thinking new ideas are weaker**: "I've already hit my top five ideas." Content is not a game of knowing - it's a game of going and finding out. Put it out and see.

3. **Format confusion**: "Do I keep doing 10-minute rants? Do I go green screen? Do I need skits?" Paralysis by analysis. Stick to 70% what you know works, 30% test.

4. **Intimidation by new followers**: When blue checkmarks and respected people start following, self-censorship creeps in. You start asking whether content is good enough for them.

5. **Perfectionism**: For 99% of creators, making content at volume to get data is the priority, not quality and perfectionism. Only prioritize quality over quantity after 100K followers, with 1-2 hours per video, and a healthy relationship with content.

## Tactical Playbook

### The Niche Vocabulary Method

The most important part of a video is NOT the hook. It is the topic - the idea.

Example: A wrestling coach went from zero to 10K in 14 days on Instagram. Why? Because of deep familiarity with wrestling community topics - knowing exactly what wrestlers want to hear discussed.

Every niche has an iceberg. At the top: generic vocabulary everyone uses (seeds, planting, soil for gardening). Below the surface: the topics elite practitioners actually discuss. If you walked into a meeting with 20 expert gardeners, what are they talking about? Hydroponics. Specific sun-ray metrics. Measurement techniques. Deep niche vocabulary.

How to find it:

1. **Reddit** - go to your niche, see what people actually discuss
2. **Time in niche (TIN)** - the more time in the niche, the more you understand
3. **Polls** - ask layered, deep questions on Stories (not "what do you want to hear about?")
4. **Move the buzzword into your hook** - literally put that niche vocabulary in the on-screen text

People don't really care about "going viral" as a topic - broad viral content doesn't perform as well as content targeting the specific problem creators have: a big following they can't monetize. That's the below-the-iceberg topic.

### Testing Formats: The 70/20/10 Split

Pulled from inside a leading creator team:

- **70%**: Guaranteed winners pulled from data. Formats and topics you know will work.
- **20%**: Improvements. Take what works and try to make it better.
- **10%**: Wild card. Test something completely new.

### Post, Analyze, Iterate

Consistency does not matter for the algorithm - it matters for you to improve. Posting consistently, if done right, will make you a beast at content, but not because the algorithm is trained.

The baseball analogy: a pitcher has certain pitches on a different count. A team gathers data for seven innings. By the seventh inning, they know if he has an 0-1 count, he's probably throwing a curveball. The same is true for content. Post, analyze three things (was it the idea? the format? what were people saying in comments?), innovate, and post again. All you're doing is speeding up your feedback loop.

### It's Okay to Be Different (Actually, It's Required)

Copying people is no longer working because market cycles in content are moving so fast. Bring unique formats, perspectives, and associations from outside your niche.

Study businesses and apply to content: the same way Apple became a billion-dollar company is the same way a creator can become a billion-viewed creator. You're both vying for trust, attention, and potentially money. Consuming the Founders Podcast by David Senra and pulling principles from Steve Jobs, Elon Musk, Sam Walton, then applying them to content strategy - that's the edge that cross-domain learning creates.

## Common Mistakes

### Changing Your Niche Too Often

Quit changing your niche. You will never be great if you keep doing it. You will lose all your compounding results. Every niche change resets your compound interest.

### Aspirational Content as Default

Quit trying to make people want to be like you. Lifestyle content, day-in-the-life without uniqueness, documenting your mundane gym visits - these do not work. Millions of people have mildly decent bodies online.

### Copying Big Creators

Their content works because of their context (massive following, team, earned trust). Copying their current output misses what they did at 0-10K to earn that position.

### Not Investing in Skill Development

If you do not have the skill set of short-form content, nothing else matters. Your niche doesn't matter. How often you should post doesn't matter. Monetization doesn't matter. You need to be able to consistently hit 1,000 views per video 3-4 times a week before anything else matters.
