# On-Screen Text as the Primary Hook Driver in Short-Form Video

## Core Insight

On-screen text carries the majority of a hook's effectiveness - significantly more than the spoken words alone. The visual text is what viewers process first, and if it fails to compel them within the first moments, the verbal content rarely recovers the watch. This means treating on-screen text as a serious copywriting discipline rather than a caption afterthought.

The principle extends beyond aesthetics. Font selection, word density, line structure, and display duration all affect whether a viewer's attention is captured and held. The two acceptable font approaches are either the platform's native font (which blends naturally into the feed context) or a deliberate luxury/brand font that signals intentionality. Anything in between reads as amateur and undermines perceived credibility before a word is processed.

## Key Tactics

- Eliminate function words entirely from on-screen text. Every word should carry semantic weight - prioritize proper nouns and action verbs over adjectives and connective filler.
- Limit display text to two lines maximum, and break lines at meaningful syntactic boundaries rather than at character-count limits.
- Keep the on-screen text visible for approximately 10 seconds - far longer than most creators hold it. Viewers need time to read, look at the visual, and re-read while also processing audio.
- Apply one of five copywriting frames to the hook text: curiosity (teasing something revealed later), pain point (naming a specific fear of the ideal viewer), dream outcome (a concrete, specific result - not vague income figures), tangibility (something the viewer can viscerally imagine holding or experiencing), or the whisper technique (parenthetical text that pre-handles objections or builds secondary curiosity).
- When using the whisper technique, identify the single biggest reason a viewer might scroll past and address it directly within the parenthetical, functioning as a mini risk-reversal.

## Why It Works

Viewers on short-form platforms are processing multiple signals simultaneously - audio, movement, and text. On-screen text acts as the cognitive anchor because it is the most controllable and static element. When the text is cluttered, poorly timed, or filled with low-value words, the brain deprioritizes it. When it is sparse, keyword-dense, and held long enough to register, it pulls attention into alignment with the audio message and dramatically increases the probability that the viewer transitions from passive scroll to active watch.
