# Eliminating Functional Words from On-Screen Text to Improve Hook Rate

## Core Insight

The source video introduces a principle called digital reading behavior - the idea that how people process text on a screen during a fast scroll is fundamentally different from how they read in other contexts. Online readers scan for keywords, take mental screenshots, and extract meaning in fragments rather than sequentially processing full sentences. This has a direct consequence for on-screen text and hook copy: words that serve grammatical function but carry no informational weight (called "functional words") consume limited screen space and viewer attention while contributing nothing to comprehension or stop-scroll impact.

The principle is quantified through analysis of real video performance: a video with more than 50% functional words in its on-screen hook underperformed significantly, while a video with under 35% functional words became a top performer. This framing turns hook writing from a creative exercise into an optimization problem with a measurable variable.

## Key Tactics

- **Audit on-screen text for functional word density**: Count the proportion of words in a hook that are pronouns, prepositions, articles, or conjunctions (words like "I," "the," "in," "and," "a," "at"). Above 50% is a strong predictor of poor hook rate.
- **Cut or restructure hooks to reduce functional words below one-third**: Rewriting a hook often means removing the subject entirely and leading with the action or concept. "How to" constructions are more efficient than "I'm going to show you how to."
- **Avoid repeating the same content keyword in the same hook**: Using the same key term twice in a short piece of on-screen text wastes the informational density that limited space allows.
- **Use typographic pattern interrupts to compensate for necessary functional words**: Parentheses, quotation marks, and formatting breaks draw attention to specific words and create visual stopping points in otherwise monotonous text streams. A viewer mid-scroll who hits a parenthetical pauses reflexively.
- **Evaluate hooks by imagining a viewer taking a screenshot**: If the screenshot of the on-screen text conveys the video's core value, the hook is working. If it reads as incomplete or ambiguous without audio or context, it needs revision.

## Why It Works

Short-form platform hooks compete in an environment where the viewer has already seen dozens of pieces of content in the previous minutes. The decision to stop or swipe happens in a fraction of a second. Hooks dense with functional words force the viewer to process more words to extract the same amount of meaning - and in the moment of that processing delay, the default behavior is to keep scrolling. Functional word reduction is essentially a signal-to-noise ratio improvement applied to the most time-critical moment in a video.
